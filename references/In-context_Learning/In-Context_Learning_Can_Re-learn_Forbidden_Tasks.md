# Abstract
Despite significant investment into safety training, large language models (LLMs) deployed in the real world still suffer from numerous vulnerabilities. One perspective on LLM safety training is that it algorithmically forbids the model from answering toxic or harmful queries. To assess the effectiveness of safety training, in this work, we study forbidden tasks, i.e., tasks the model is designed to refuse to answer. Specifically, we investigate whether in-context learning (ICL) can be used to re-learn forbidden tasks despite the explicit fine-tuning of the model to refuse them. We first examine a toy example of refusing sentiment classification to demonstrate the problem. Then, we use ICL on a model fine-tuned to refuse to summarise made-up news articles. Finally, we investigate whether ICL can undo safety training, which could represent a major security risk. For the safety task, we look at VICUNA-7B, STARLING-7B, and LLAMA2-7B. We show that the attack works out-of-the-box on STARLING7B and VICUNA-7B but fails on LLAMA2-7B. Finally, we propose an ICL attack that uses the chat template tokens like a prompt injection attack to achieve a better attack success rate on VICUNA7B and STARLING-7B. With the ever growing capabilities of large language models (LLMs) comes an increasing concern about how to make them safe (Touvron et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2022) and prevent bad behaviours (Amodei et al., 2016). These issues, together with the model’s power to create harmful outputs, have created a need for safety research to prevent malicious behaviours like hallucination and answering harmful queries. The most common training pipeline for creating safe and helpful LLMs is to take a pre-trained base model that was optimised on the next token prediction and to fine-tune it to be helpful and safe (Brown et al., 2020; Touvron et al., 2023; OpenAI et al., 2023). This fine-tuning typically involves applying a mixture of techniques on high-quality data from user chat examples, human preference data, or data generated by high-quality models. The techniques used are numerous, but the most common ones include instruction fine-tuning (Longpre et al., 2023), reinforcement learning methods such as proximal policy optimisation (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) together with reward modelling (Bai et al., 2022), or direct preference optimisation (Rafailov et al., 2023). However, as a model is fine-tuned to be safer, it can become less helpful or effective at other tasks, necessitating a careful balance with the techniques and the data used. However, it remains a key challenge to evaluate whether these “safety fine-tuning” approaches sufficiently minimize harm, especially against determined users. Conventionally, claims like the robustness or reliability of a deep learning model against an adversary are evaluated with adversarial attacks (Szegedy et al., 2014; Carlini & Wagner, 2017). Currently, in natural language processing (NLP) there is no agreed upon threat model unlike other fields such as computer vision (Akhtar & Mian, 2018) where the perturbations to the input image have to be very small. A possible threat-model allows the attacker full access to the model’s internals, including the weights. With access to the weights, the attacker may use the model gradients to optimize the token in a prompt that would bypass the model’s safety guardrails and cause it to output harmful content (Zou et al., 2023). However, another (potentially emergent) behavior of LLMs—their ability to learn in-context (Radford et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2020; Olsson et al., 2022)—might be a vulnerability. In-context learning, also known as few-shot prompting, involves learning to predict the next token for an unseen query based on a few example mappings shown in-context, the input to an LLM. Crucially, in-context learning (ICL) does not involve gradient updates. As such, ICL could be a possible “vector of attack” by which a malicious user teaches an LLM in-context to produce harmful outputs, undoing some of the effects of safety fine-tuning.
Given the potential negative impact of ICL, this paper develops a framework for studying the effects of in-context learning on “unlearning” the trained behaviour of a model. More specifically, models in the context of safety are trained to refuse to answer certain queries and tasks, which we call a forbidden task. We investigate in a more systematic manner to what extent ICL is able to circumvent the fine-tuning to refuse. A recent work (Wei et al., 2023b) also studied the effects of ICL on undoing safety fine-tuning. They focused on attacking a single model to decrease its harmlessness, and studied using ICL examples at inference time as a defense against gradient-based attacks. In contrast, in this paper, we study the question of in-context learning attacks beyond just harmful content generation and test these ideas on multiple LLMs. Our conclusions regarding safety are more nuanced than the ones of (Wei et al., 2023b) as we show that the ICL attack does not work universally. We propose a specific kind of ICL attack we call the In-Chat ICL attack, which requires the knowledge of the {User} and {Assistant} chat template tokens to craft the ICL attack. In our work, we first look at sentiment classification as a forbidden task and fine-tune a model to refuse to answer sentiment classification queries. This methodology mimics the procedure of producing a specialised language model, e.g. a chatbot of a company, that needs to refuse certain tasks. We then attack the model by teaching it to classify sentiment with in-context examples. For a second forbidden task, we train the model to refuse to summarise news articles based on a weblink, which it cannot do as our models do not have access to the internet. Finally, we experiment with VICUNA-7B, STARLING-7B, and LLAMA2-7B to evaluate whether ICL can break the safety alignment of LLMs. We show that on VICUNA-7B and STARLING-7B ICL does increase the attack success rate. Although we find that on LLAMA2-7B the ICL attack fails, the log-probability of an informative response increases. Further, we show that In-Chat ICL is much better at breaking the safety alignment of LLMs. To summarise, our contributions are:
 We demonstrate on two tasks that LLAMA2-70B can re-learn tasks via in-context learning that it was finetuned to refuse and show a correlation in the attack success rate between different tasks depending on the ICL example token length.
 We demonstrate on two tasks that LLAMA2-70B can re-learn tasks via in-context learning that it was finetuned to refuse and show a correlation in the attack success rate between different tasks depending on the ICL example token length.
• We introduce In-chat ICL attacks and show that the safety behavior of VICUNA-7B and STARLING-7B is vulnerable to ICL and In-chat ICL attacks, while LLAMA2-7B is resilient.

# 2. Background
2.1. In-context learning
# 2.1. In-context learning
Prompting refers to giving the model a list of tokens from which it then autoregressively continues to generate new tokens. In-context learning, also known as few-shot prompting, occurs when we give several input-output pairs in a prompt before we ask for a different example of interest (Brown et al., 2020). If we have examples xi with answers yi then an in-context learning prompt will look like
[x0]; [y0]; . . . ; [xi]; [yi]; [xj];
where ; is concatenation and xj is the example for which we actually desire the answer yj. This does not involve any gradient updates, and instead happens at inference time.
# 2.2. Hallucination
LLMs are prone to what we call hallucinations or confabulations (Guerreiro et al., 2023; Millidge, 2023; Bottou & Sch¨olkopf, 2023). This occurs when the language model produces statements as facts that are obviously false. Obviously false meaning that we can, with relative ease, factcheck a statement to be incorrect. For instance, a made-up reference can easily be verified to not exist. Another example might be “The earth is flat” or “The official language of the US is French”.
# 2.3. Safety & Alignment in Large Language Models
The goal of aligning large language models is to achieve the trifecta of helpful, honest, and harmless (Askell et al., 2021). Helpfulness refers to the desiderata that the model make its best attempt at fulfilling the user’s request in an efficient manner. This includes proposing alternative approaches if the user’s request is impractical. Honest means that, at the very least, the model should give accurate information including providing the corresponding level of certainty it has about a given fact. Moreover, the model should be honest about what it knows and its aim is and not try to deceive the user in any way. Harmlessness is the goal that the model should not produce toxic or discriminatory content. It also should refuse to aid in harmful task,
e.g. when a user asks how to build a bomb it should refuse. Finally, the model should be careful when it provides consequential advice. In this paper, when we talk about ‘breaking’ the safety alignment of a model we primarily mean either creating harmful examples, or creating dishonest answers by producing hallucinations.
# 3. Experimental Results
The goal of this paper is to evaluate whether in-context learning can undo the fine-tuning intended to teach a model to refuse to answer certain queries.
Given a language model f that has the ability to do in-context learning sufficiently well and solve a task T characterised by a set of prompts {xj}, we fine-tune the model f to refuse to answer T . We call the fine-tuned language model f ′. We now wish to test in-context learning’s ability to recover task performance that has been destroyed during fine-tuning by training to refuse to answer. Specifically, we want to find a prompt p such that f ′([p; x]) ≈f(x). Where p takes the form of an in-context learning prompt, i.e. p = [x0]; [y0]; . . . ; [xi]; [yi].
Throughout all experiments unless otherwise mentioned we use the LLAMA2-70B model (Touvron et al., 2023). All fine-tuning is done with either supervised fine-tuning or DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023). We also include the default system prompt of LLAMA2 to properly mimic the behaviour of a black box model. For all experiments we use PyTorch together with the Huggingface transformers library for the models, peft library for the QLoRa (Dettmers et al.) weights, and trl library for the implementation of supervised fine-tuning and DPO. We run our code on one to two NVIDIA A100 GPUs.
# 3.1. Task 1: Sentiment Classification
The first task we look at is sentiment classification. Given a phrase or excerpt of a sentence we ask the model to classify whether this example is either negative or positive in tone. In the fine-tuning stage, we train the model to refuse to answer this sentiment classification task, which it normally would perform to the best of it’s ability. The sentiment classification task serves as a proxy to show that preventing the model from performing a given task is non-trivial. Further the task illustrates that for a model to be safe, during the fine-tuning phase, it must learn to distinguish the forbidden task from all other tasks. The benefit of using sentiment classification is that since its output is binary, evaluating an LLM’s refusal compared to “positive” or “negative” is straightforward. In
contrast, for more complex queries, LLM’s outputs can be unconstrained, making it challenging to evaluate whether fine-tuning or an attack was successful. More specifically, we fine-tune the model on the SST-2 dataset with direct preference optimisation (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) where we use β = 0.25. β is a penalty term for the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the model f and the fine-tuned weights of f ′. We choose the value to be as small as possible1 to ensure that the model learns to refuse well, while preventing it from learning to refuse to answer any question. We verify this manually by asking it other unrelated questions such as “What is 5+5?” or “Who is the 45th president of the US?”. We train for 100 steps with batch size 16 and we reach convergence of the log probability of the rejected and chosen responses. See §A.2.1 for the template. We evaluate the response by counting the occurrence of the words ‘positive’ and ‘negative’. If only one word occurs we consider that the model classified the sentiment of the sentence. If both or neither occur then we consider that the model refused to classify the sentiment. Note that in the Figure 5 in the Appendix we additionally distinguish between correct and incorrect classification when they occur. In total, we evaluate on 100 held-out examples. The ICL attack uses 16 randomly chosen examples unseen during training. As we can see in Table 1, the model clearly learned to refuse sentiment classification. Instead of always performing the task it rejects to classify in over 90% of cases. However, in-context learning is able to recover the model’s answer to a significant extent. In the Appendix we verify the correctness of the predictions as well (see §A.1).
# 3.2. Task 2: Link Hallucination
The high-level idea of this task is the following: LLMs that do not have access to the internet cannot summarise a news article only provided as a link. Thus, if they do provide a summary, it must be a hallucination and instead they should be refusing the task. In other words, this is a non-toxic task that the language model should always refuse. If after fine-tuning the model does posses this guardrail, we can incontext teach the model to complete the task anyway, it will be another piece of evidence that in-context learning is able to re-learn a forbidden task. A benefit of this task is that they do not require us to produce misaligned, toxic models. An additional benefit is that it is easy to generate high-quality data given the prevalence of news article datasets.

# 4.1. Dataset
AdvBench (Chen et al., 2022) is a dataset consisting of two parts. The first part consists of pairs of a harmful prompt (typically seeking information about a harmful goal) and an affirmative response to the prompt. We give a few examples in Figure 2. The second part of the dataset consists of harmful strings, which are offensive, toxic, or discriminatory. The dataset has 520 harmful prompt-response pairs and 574 harmful strings. For the in-context examples we use the examples from Wei et al. (2023b) that include a full response that goes beyond just the affirmative response (see §A.4 Warning: the appendix contains LLM-generated text with violence, suicide, and misinformation.).
# 4.2. Models
We look at several models for the safety analysis: VICUNA7B (Zheng et al., 2023), STARLING-7B (Zhu* et al., 2023), and LLAMA2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023). VICUNA-7B is based on LLAMA2-7B but has been finetuned on userchats with ChatGPT 4. STARLING-7B is based on OpenChat (Wang et al., 2023) and has been trained to be harmless. We use LLAMA2-7B to avoid quantisation as well as having a consistent model size compared to the other models.
# 4.3. Attack prompt
We test two different attack prompts. The first is the classic ICL attack we have studied so far, which consists of a user giving several input-output examples. This looks like {User}:x0; y0; . . . ; xi; yi; x. The second, which we call In-Chat ICL attack, consists of giving the demonstrations as a previous chat between the language model and the user, i.e. {User}:x0; {Assistant}:y0; . . . :
{User}:xi; {Assistant}:yi; {User}:x. The second approach is similar to a prompt injection attack, but using ICL examples.
# 4.4. Results
We first evaluate the number of responses where, after the ICL attack, the model produces a plausibly affirmative response to a harmful query. To calculate this metric, we follow the methodology used for our other tasks and check for a refusal of the model with a list of keywords (see §A.4). We present the results in Table 2. We find that the attack somewhat succeeds on VICUNA-7B, succeeds more noticeably on STARLING-7B, but fails with LLAMA2-7B. We further note that more examples seems to help when attacking VICUNA-7B, but makes little tangible difference to STARLING-7B though this can be explained by the high attack success rate. The main difference between VICUNA7B and LLAMA2-7B is that the former has received further fine-tuning on user chats with ChatGPT (Zheng et al., 2023). This implies that extensive safety fine-tuning that LLAMA27B has received has been forgotten by VICUNA-7B to some extent. This demonstrates a need for safety fine-tuning when specialising a model. STARLING-7B on the other hand is based on OPENCHAT (Wang et al., 2023), which in turn is based on MISTRAL-7B (Jiang et al., 2023). OPENCHAT has no safety training and instead the harmlessness is added by the fine-tuning done for STARLING-7B. However, as Table 2 shows this is quite weak and largely circumvented with some in-context examples. For the In-Chat ICL attacks we observe a large improvement in attack success rate for VICUNA-7B and a smaller improvement for STARLING-7B. For LLAMA2-7B, on the other hand, we see no change at all. For VICUNA-7B the attack success rate increases significantly with more examples. # 4.5. Limitations
The log probability analysis has the key limitation that is only looks at a single token’s probability. It could well be, as is the case for STARLING-7B, that a different token than the one we look at will be indicative of an affirmative or negative response.

For the safety task we have a more nuanced picture. The attack is successful against VICUNA-7B and STARLING7B suggesting that indeed the ICL attack trends we saw in the sentiment classification and link hallucination tasks are indeed predictive of what occurs when using ICL attacks to undo safety alignment. However, the attack fails on LLAMA2-7B implying with more extensive fine-tuning, defending against such ICL attacks is possible. Looking more closely at the log probability of an affirmative response we can see that even if the attack fails to generate toxic content on LLAMA2-7B we are still able to make a harmful response more likely. Thus, if the trend observed in Figure 4 continues with better and more examples, an ICL attack may eventually be successful. In addition, given that VICUNA7B is a fine-tuned model based on LLAMA2-7B and is vulnerable to an ICL attack this implies that the fine-tuning likely weakened the safety features of the model. This forgetting is important for practitioners to be aware of. We look at the In-Chat ICL attack to study a more powerful attack format. The In-Chat ICL setting is a form of prompt injection–as it requires the knowledge of the {User} and {Assistant} chat tokens template to craft the ICL attack– and thus is more restrictive than the classic ICL setting which we first considered. Nonetheless, we argue that attack format is worth studying as an attacker might find ways to control the prompt sufficiently to perform ICL In-Chat. Moreover, the difference in performance for VICUNA-7B In-Chat and STARLING-7B In-Chat suggests that the model is possibly better at learning in context than we thought.
# 6. Related Work
# 6.1. Prompting & In-context learning
Several papers have studied prompting mechanisms to teach a LLM a new task. Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2023a; Yao et al., 2023; Long, 2023), scratchpads (Nye et al., 2021), and in-context learning (Brown et al., 2020; Olsson et al., 2022) being the most common. All of these prompting mechanism have in common to improve the performance of a model on a given task. In this paper we focus on ICL as it is the most promising to teach new general tasks rather than focusing on reasoning tasks.
# 6.2. Fine-tuning & Safety training
The most common fine-tuning method is instruction finetuning or also called supervised fine-tuning (Longpre et al., 2023). It has been shown to improve generalisation (Wei et al., 2022; Sanh et al., 2022; Ivison et al., 2023) and alignment (Zhou et al., 2023).
In particular for safety concerns reinforcement learning methods have become more popular as a way to fine-tune models. RLHF (Bai et al., 2022) and RLAIF (Lee et al.,
In particular for safety concerns reinforcement learning methods have become more popular as a way to fine-tune models. RLHF (Bai et al., 2022) and RLAIF (Lee et al.,
2023) have been the most popular methods training a reward model and then using PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) to tune the model on the learned reward models. DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) combines the training of a reward model and the reinforcement learning steps into a single optimisation step.
While adversarial attacks have a clear definition in the vision domain (Szegedy et al., 2014; Carlini & Wagner, 2017), the threat model and objective for natural language processing (NLP) have yet to be agreed upon by the community. An objective closely inspired by the vision domain attempts to attack LLMs with the goal that they misclassify certain inputs (Min et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2023). Here the threat model is that the attacker may change the string as little as possible to achieve this misclassification. A different objective is to break the model by making it output text that is unaligned, i.e. the string is harmful or dishonest or otherwise unsafe. Adversarial attacks for this objective against LLMs have taken a variety of forms. Broadly they can be categorised into black-box attacks, i.e. no access to the model insides, and white-box attacks, where attackers do have access to the model weights. In the line of work on white-box attacks, Zou et al. (2023) modifies the AUTOPROMPT method (Shin et al., 2020) to use the gradient of the model to automatically find a prompt to generate the affirmative response. Ding et al. (2023) uses automatic prompt rewriting with the most common attacking tricks and scenario nesting, whereby the prompt is hidden within a made up scenario, to attack LLMs in a black-box setting. Very recent work Wei et al. (2023b) also considered ICL examples to attack VICUNA-7B. In comparison to their paper, our analysis is much more extensive and our conclusion more nuanced; we look at two different tasks as well as attacking LLAMA2-7B, showing that ICL attack is not universal. Finally, we investigate the changes in log-probability, which give additional insights into the attack. Their work instead focuses on in-context defense examples in the prompt to defend against in-context attacks. Several works (Yang et al., 2023; Qi et al., 2023) have also shown the ease with which one can fine-tune models to be helpful, but harmful with only very little data. Datasets for the task of producing adversarial attacks against LLMs are include AdvBench (Chen et al., 2022) which provides examples of harmful requests that should be denied together with an affirmative response. A second dataset is the red teaming dataset from Ganguli et al. (2022). This dataset consists of transcripts of red teaming attempts. Each red team attempt is scored on how harmful the output from the model is and the target given to the crowd-workers.

# 7. Conclusion
In this paper, we have shown that a LLM that has been fine-tuned to a refuse a task can re-learn that task in-context. More specifically, the sentiment classification task and the link hallucination task results show a clear story that across the two most common fine-tuning methods, SFT and DPO, that forbidden tasks can be re-learned. Furthermore, the same in-context learning attack is able to break the safety guardrails of VICUNA-7B and STARLING-7B. This implies that safety checks should consider the in-context learning attack vector. In addition, the qualitative and quantitative similarity across the three settings suggests this training to refuse is an alternative lens to study safety training. The failure of the attack against LLAMA2-7B to generate harmful content suggests defense is possible, however, even against LLAMA2-7B the attack is able to increase the probability of an affirmative response, thus implying that vigilance against and awareness of this vector of attack is necessary. Finally, our semantic analysis suggests that more similar queries are not necessarily better in-context examples. We leave it to future work to investigate if more and better examples can continue increasing the likelihood of an affirmative response on LLAMA2-7B or whether this plateaus before reaching the point of generating a harmful response.
# 8. Broader Impact Statement
This work studies the safety and vulnerability of large language model. This is necessary to create ethical artificial intelligent assistants. Thus our paper has a direct impact and helps practitioners be aware of the risks of their models. While the ideas of this study may also be used to attack models to generate harmful content, we believe that it is necessary to investigate these vectors of attacks so that safer language models can be built and that this outweighs the negatives. This line of work can help raise awareness for legislation, regulation, and deployment of large language models. Our work links safety research to refusing a task, thus potentially making future safety research safer by providing alternatives to toxic benchmarks.

# A.2. Training with in-context examples
So far we have shown that in-context learning can be used to attack LLMs. So the question naturally arises if we train on in-context examples, does this prevent them breaking our alignment? A second follow-up question is at what cost, i.e. does training to refuse in-context examples decrease our ability to in-context learn new different tasks that were not intended. To test this we go back to the sentiment classification task and instead of training on simple sentiment classification we train on the ICL examples we used to break the model and the previous data.
We again fine-tune with DPO and use 8 or 4 ICL examples for half of the training data during training. We test on both the original SST-2 dataset as well as the unrelated task in GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021). GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) is a high-school math problem benchmark on which the community commonly evaluates both problem solving capabilities and ICL abilities. For the GSM8K we follow the LLAMA2 paper few-shot set-up with 8 examples2. We train with both β = 0.2 and β = 0.25 to show that allowing the model stray away too far from the reference model leads to a model that refuses to answer any prompt. We show the results in Table 3. At the lower β value of 0.2 we can clearly see that the effectiveness of ICL attacks is strongly reduced. Using longer ICL examples during training can help as we can see from difference in recovered performance between training on 8 or 4 ICL examples. Disappointingly, the model has learned to refuse so strongly at this value that the performance on GSM8K has decreased to nearly 0. At the higher β value of 0.25 we can see that learning to refuse on in-context examples has not worked. This shows that using ICL training data alone during fine-tuning does not help in defending against this vector of attack.
